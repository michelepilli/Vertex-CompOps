Foreword

Indirect tax is one of the few functions in an enterprise where determinations are made continuously, the exposure is lasting, and the conversation is still episodic and after the fact.

Every transaction a company completes carries a tax determination — even an exempt sale requires a decision that it is exempt, and usually a certificate to prove it. Most are made by software, in milliseconds, without a person reviewing them. They add up to a position the company will one day have to defend — to a tax authority, an auditor, occasionally a customer who noticed something first. Yet at most enterprises, the people accountable for those determinations reach the board only when something has already surfaced.

We set out to test whether that matters. We believe the working relationship between tax, finance, and IT is central to how well indirect tax and compliance function. Vertex calls this discipline Compliance Operations (CompOps™): continuously integrating tax, IT, finance, and audit response into a coordinated operating rhythm. So to answer the question of what distinguishes one enterprise from the next in its tax and compliance posture, we asked 750 tax, finance, and IT professionals across global enterprises how and where indirect tax and compliance fits within their strategic and operating agendas. We looked at the question two ways. First, we categorized finance and tax leaders by how often indirect tax reaches their board or executive committee. Second, we segmented all 750 enterprises into four tiers of AI capability in indirect tax and compliance. Then we asked what differs between the groups on outcomes neither arrangement was built on: where errors surface, how fast tax authorities get answers, how long a tax change takes to go live.

Both lenses point the same way, and neither is explained by company size. Enterprises that govern indirect tax differently tend to run it differently. They catch more of their own errors and rely less on chance and on making tax changes by hand. Where indirect tax reaches the board regularly, they also answer tax authorities faster. This is a study of one point in time, so it cannot prove which comes first. But the pattern is consistent, and it is not a matter of scale.

We have also published what the data does not support. Enterprises that govern indirect tax closely report no fewer returns needing rework and spend no fewer hours on manual compliance work than those that do not. But critically, they are more likely to find their errors themselves. And about half of all enterprises still take the more conservative tax treatment when they cannot prove a position — whatever their governance or their AI capability. Even enterprises that review indirect tax as a standing board item are more likely than others to say that half or more of their tax reserve exists because they cannot readily prove the position, not because the law is unclear.

For the more than four in ten enterprises where indirect tax reaches the board only when a problem arises, if at all, the opportunity is to put it on the agenda before one does.

For everyone, the gap that remains is proving the integrity of the original tax decision — especially when it must be defended under scrutiny.

We present this Index to help enterprises see how their indirect tax and compliance posture compares with that of their peers, so that their decisions can be proved and their outcomes improved over time.

Allison Cerra
Chief Marketing Officer, Vertex, Inc.

The seven findings

  1. #1
    Governance
    Indirect tax largely has no standing seat at the board.

    Only 16% of enterprises discuss indirect tax and compliance as a standing board item. More than two in five (43%) do so only when a problem arises or rarely, if ever.

  2. #2
    Governance
    Board-engaged enterprises catch more of their own errors and answer tax authorities faster.

    Board-engaged enterprises report significantly better outcomes than board-absent enterprises. They are one-quarter as likely to learn of their errors from an external party (4% vs. 16%), nearly twice as likely to answer a tax-authority notice within five business days (48% vs. 26%), and half as likely to make and check taxability changes entirely by hand (13% vs. 26%).

  3. #3
    Capability
    Enterprises with more advanced AI capability in indirect tax report better outcomes beyond the capability score.

    Nearly one in four of the least capable enterprises (23%) say an external party most often identifies their errors, and one in five (20%) needs more than 30 business days to put a taxability change live. In the most capable tier (n=34), no respondent reports either.

  4. #4
    Capability
    Each step up the tiers involves a different discipline.

    Moving from the least capable tier (Tier 1) to Tier 2 is associated with making and checking fewer tax changes entirely by hand. Advancing from Tier 2 to Tier 3 goes with a deliberate governance approach. Moving from Tier 3 to the most capable tier is accompanied by consolidating tax rules in a dedicated tax engine and absorbing compliance mandates faster.

  5. #5
    Capability
    The market’s AI vocabulary outruns its AI.

    Despite respondents seeing a precise definition, no more than 45% of self-described AI agent workflows actually qualify. This gap reflects market terminology confusion rather than intentional deception. Consequently, self-reported stats require scrutiny. To address this, the study uses a ten-question capability score, measuring relative performance across tiers rather than relying on any single figure at face value.

  6. #6
    Cross-lens
    Neither lens is explained by company size.

    Size does not explain governance or capability. Surprisingly, large enterprises (10,000+ employees) comprise 34% of the least capable tier, despite being only 18% of all organizations studied. What separates enterprises is how they prioritize and run indirect tax — for example, whether IT funds it as a strategic priority — rather than how big they are.

  7. #7
    Cross-lens
    Proving the position remains unsolved.

    Regardless of board cadence or tier, about half take the conservative treatment when they cannot prove a position. And among enterprises holding indirect tax reserves, nearly three-quarters say at least a quarter of the reserve exists because they cannot readily demonstrate the position, rather than because the law is unclear.

What the two lenses say together

Seen through either lens, the enterprises that treat indirect tax as a strategic matter look different in daily operation. The two lenses overlap: 13% of board-engaged enterprises sit in the least capable tier, against 34% of board-absent enterprises. Capability accounts for some of the difference between board-engaged and board-absent organizations, but not all of it. Among developing and advanced enterprises, those that discuss indirect tax regularly at the board level still report better outcomes on several measures.

The limits are just as clear. Governance and capability correspond to catching more errors internally and, on the governance lens, answering faster. Governance does not correspond to fewer returns needing rework or fewer manual hours. And neither lens changes how often enterprises take the conservative treatment when they cannot prove a position.

Why indirect tax governance matters now

This report is framed by two compounding pressures, concepts developed by Vertex. The first is Defensibility Drift™: the gap between the decisions a business makes at the point of transaction and its ability to prove those outcomes to authorities across an accelerating patchwork of jurisdictions. The second is Compliance Operations (CompOps) Drag™: the manual rework, integration friction, and delayed audit response created by fragmented tax, ERP, e-commerce, and reporting systems that were never designed to run continuous compliance in real time.

The two differ in origin — drift arrives from outside, drag is built inside — but they compound. A mandate arrives that the organization cannot absorb quickly. The workaround it adopts adds another seam to the architecture. The next mandate is harder still. Left ungoverned, drift and drag compound into a vicious cycle of complexity. Governed end to end, from determination to defense, the same lifecycle compounds the other way — into a virtuous cycle of control, where outcomes are accurate and audits are answerable. Vertex's Decision-to-Defense™ approach treats every determination as an outcome to prove, defend, and improve, all governed as one continuous flow across the transaction lifecycle.

The financial consequence of unmitigated drift and drag is what Vertex calls the Compliance Confidence Gap™: the distance between what enterprises could book, claim, or release from reserves with defensible confidence and what they conservatively report, forgo, or hold in reserve because they cannot prove every determination. This study measures both sides of it — how often enterprises take the more conservative treatment when they cannot prove a position, and how much of their reserves exist for the same reason (Finding 7).

Both pressures are governance problems before they are technology problems. An organization that reviews indirect tax only when something surfaces has no mechanism for noticing either pressure until it has already produced a consequence. That is why this report starts with the board.

Sidebar 1 — External context

What drift looks like on a calendar

Every mandate below has a date, and each requires system change by that date.

European Union. The VAT in the Digital Age package (ViDA) was adopted by the Council on March 11, 2025, and entered into force on April 14, 2025. Single VAT registration reforms and platform rules follow from July 1, 2028. From July 1, 2030, cross-border business-to-business sales within the EU require structured e-invoices and near-real-time digital reporting. Member states with older domestic reporting systems must align them with the EU model by January 1, 2035.

National mandates are moving faster. Germany has required businesses to be able to receive e-invoices since January 1, 2025. Poland’s KSeF became mandatory for large taxpayers on February 1, 2026, and for other VAT-registered businesses on April 1, 2026, with micro-businesses following in January 2027. France requires every business to receive e-invoices, and large and mid-sized companies to issue them, from September 1, 2026; small businesses follow on September 1, 2027. Romania, which has cleared business-to-business invoices centrally since July 2024, extended reporting to consumer sales in January 2025.

For an enterprise selling into several of these markets, each date is drift arriving from outside. The workaround it adopts when it cannot absorb a mandate cleanly is drag. The calendar is why the two compound rather than alternate.

This is external context describing the environment enterprises operate in. It is not a finding of this study.

Part I — The governance lens

Finance and tax leaders were asked how often indirect tax and compliance is discussed at their board or executive-committee level. This part of the report compares two groups built from their answers:

Board-engaged

Indirect tax is a standing item or is discussed periodically (57% of finance and tax respondents, n=213). Where this report says indirect tax reaches the board “regularly,” it means this group.

Board-absent

Indirect tax comes up only when a specific problem arises, or rarely or never (43%, n=156).

Only finance and tax leaders answered this question, so every figure in Part I is on that base. IT leaders did not answer it, and IT-only measures cannot be compared by board cadence.

Finding 1 · Governance lens

Indirect tax largely has no standing seat at the board

Only one in six enterprises (16%) reviews indirect tax as a standing board or executive-committee item. For 43%, it reaches that level only when a problem arises, or rarely at all.

Across the finance and tax leaders surveyed, 15.7% say indirect tax is a standing item reviewed on a regular cadence. Another 41.5% say it is discussed periodically, as part of broader finance or risk updates. For 32.4% it comes up only when a specific problem or event arises — an audit, a penalty, a major regulatory change — and for 10.5% it is rarely or never discussed at that level.

Put differently, the board reviews indirect tax as a standing item at a minority of enterprises, and at more than four in ten it sees the function only after something has already gone wrong or is about to.

Finding 2 · Governance lens

Board-engaged enterprises catch more of their own errors and answer tax authorities faster

Compared with board-absent enterprises, board-engaged enterprises are one-quarter as likely to learn of their errors from an external party, nearly twice as likely to answer a tax-authority notice within five business days, and half as likely to make and check taxability changes entirely by hand.

The two groups run their operations differently on almost every measure the study captured. Board-engaged enterprises most often catch errors through automated controls or AI (63% vs. 35%) and are half as likely to rely on someone noticing by chance (10% vs. 19%). When a tax rule must change, they are half as likely to make and check the change entirely by hand (13% vs. 26%), and far less likely to need more than 30 business days to put it live (2% vs. 11%). When a regulation changes, 61% have a defined or proactive process to catch it before it takes effect, against 38%.

The difference shows up in how enterprises face the tax authority, too. Board-engaged enterprises are one-quarter as likely to say an external party — a tax authority, auditor, customer, or vendor — most often finds their errors (4% vs. 16%), and nearly twice as likely to answer a tax-authority notice within five business days (48% vs. 26%).

Two cautions apply. Board-engaged respondents are more likely to be C-suite (40% vs. 29%), so some of the gap may reflect who answered. And board-engaged enterprises are concentrated in the upper capability tiers (13% sit in the least capable tier, against 34% of board-absent enterprises), which may account for some of the difference.

The gap extends to AI

Board-engaged enterprises are also further along with AI in indirect tax. Among them, 54% say AI now operates autonomously or handles routine work in their tax workflows, against 21% of board-absent enterprises. They are more likely to have a dedicated budget (63% vs. 45%) and a named executive owner (61% vs. 41%) for AI in indirect tax, to monitor AI accuracy after deployment (64% vs. 44%), and to report significant or transformational value from their AI (48% vs. 31%). They are also more likely to convene a standing tax, IT, and finance forum on a defined cadence (56% vs. 40%).

All these measures are also inputs to the AI Capability Score in Part II, and board-engaged enterprises are concentrated in the upper tiers. The data in this section describes the governance lens; it is not independent evidence for it.

Sidebar 2 — External context

The two directions an external party can catch

The survey’s external-party option combines tax authority, auditor, customer, and vendor, so the study cannot say which of them most often found the error. But they differ in a way that shapes what external detection costs. Tax authorities and auditors typically surface under-collection. Customers surface over-collection, because they hold the receipt.

Over-collection can turn a tax matter into a consumer-protection matter. In the United States, customers charged tax on exempt goods, or at the wrong rate, have brought class actions under state consumer-protection statutes, and state attorneys general have pursued retailers over the same practice. The remedy can include refunds, penalties, and legal costs that exceed the tax itself.

The same failure to detect produces materially different consequences depending on who notices first.

This is external context describing the environment enterprises operate in. It is not a finding of this study.

The surprising lack of differences

There are two key areas where no statistically significant differences are found. Board-engaged enterprises report no fewer returns needing rework after the first pass (26% vs. 27% say more than one in ten) and no fewer manual hours (55% vs. 57% spend 100 or more hours a month on manual compliance work). Governance does not appear to mean fewer errors or fewer manual hours. It corresponds to catching more of those errors internally, answering for them faster, and relying less on chance.

The propensity for an enterprise to feature indirect tax on the board agenda more regularly also does not differ significantly by size. Enterprises with more than $1 billion in revenue, and those with 10,000 or more employees, make up statistically similar shares of both the board-engaged and board-absent groups.

Finding 3 · Capability lens

Enterprises with more advanced AI capability report meaningfully better outcomes — on measures the score does not include

On measures that play no part in the questions used to identify the tiers — where errors surface, how long a tax change takes to go live, how quickly mandates are absorbed, how regulatory change is caught — the least and most capable tiers look like different operations.

The study did not measure how often errors occur. It measured who most often catches them, where a material difference was found. Nearly one in four of the least capable enterprises (23%) say an external party most often finds their indirect tax errors. Among the most capable (n=34), none do.

There is also a significant difference in the amount of time required to effectuate tax changes. One in five of the least capable needs more than 30 business days to put a single taxability change live across its systems; among both the advanced and most capable tiers, none does.

Proactive, automated monitoring of regulatory change is uncommon in the two lower tiers (9% and 10%), then rises to 34% at Tier 3 and 55% at Tier 4. IT leaders at the most capable enterprises are the most likely to absorb a new e-invoicing or real-time reporting mandate in under a month (27%, against 7–10% in every other tier), and 85% describe indirect tax as a funded strategic priority on the IT roadmap, against 5% in the least capable tier.

The gap in answering a tax-authority notice within five business days, while large, is not statistically significant across tiers (30% at Tier 1, 57% at Tier 4). That said, as shown in Exhibit 2, it is significant when viewed through the governance lens.

Europe and the United States: where capability sits

Europe and the United States do not differ significantly on board cadence (20.6% vs. 13.8% standing item), which is why this comparison sits with the capability lens rather than the governance lens. They do differ on execution. European enterprises are significantly less likely to fund indirect tax as a strategic IT priority, to monitor regulatory change proactively, or to run a standing tax, IT, and finance forum — and significantly more likely to keep tax rules hard-coded in ERP customizations or spreadsheets. They are also, directionally, more likely to sit in the least capable tier (25% vs. 18%). The regulatory expectation is highest where the operating discipline is lower (see Sidebar 3).

Sidebar 3 — External context

When it’s personal

The United Kingdom’s Senior Accounting Officer regime, introduced under Schedule 46 of the Finance Act 2009, requires large companies to name a senior officer — typically the chief financial officer — who must certify annually to HM Revenue & Customs that the company maintains appropriate tax accounting arrangements. Value Added Tax, customs duties, and excise duties are explicitly within scope, alongside corporation tax, payroll taxes, and stamp taxes. The regime applies to UK-incorporated companies with turnover above £200 million or a balance sheet above £2 billion, aggregated across the UK group.

The accountability is personal and cannot be delegated to an adviser. Failure to comply carries a £5,000 penalty on the named individual, as does failing to provide a timely certificate or providing one with a careless or deliberate inaccuracy. The penalties attach to the officer’s duty across every tax in scope, not to indirect tax specifically — but because VAT sits within that scope, a control failure in indirect tax can produce a personal penalty for the officer who signed.

Supranational guidance points the same way. The OECD’s guidance on tax control frameworks, adopted by several revenue authorities, holds the board accountable for the design, implementation, and effectiveness of the framework.

Read alongside Exhibits 1 and 8, the implication is uncomfortable. Board-level attention to indirect tax is a minority practice, and European enterprises lag on execution in a region where at least one major tax authority already presumes that governance is in place.

This is external context describing the environment enterprises operate in. It is not a finding of this study.

Finding 4 · Capability lens

Each step up the tiers involves a different discipline

Moving up the tiers, enterprises rely less on manual effort, govern indirect tax more deliberately, and absorb regulatory change faster. Each step has its own signature.

01

The difference between Tier 1 and Tier 2:
reduced manual execution

Almost half of Tier 1 finance and tax leaders (45%) say taxability changes are made and checked entirely by hand, against 13% at Tier 2. One in five of the least capable enterprises needs more than 30 business days to put a change live, against 5% at Tier 2. And four in ten Tier 1 IT leaders say the tax rules are spread across systems with no single source of truth, or hard-coded into ERP customizations and spreadsheets, against one in six at Tier 2.

The least capable tier is also the one that expands AI without evidence of benefit. Among enterprises that have extended AI beyond a first workflow, 16% of Tier 1 required measured, documented time savings first, against about half in every tier above. Like its more advanced counterparts, the least capable tier checks that AI is accurate; unlike them, it rarely checks that AI has paid off.

02

The difference between Tier 2 and Tier 3:
increased governance

This is where the two lenses meet most visibly in the data. Standing board attention barely moves between Tier 1 and Tier 2 (6% to 7%, both significantly below average). It more than triples to 24% at Tier 3. Also between Tier 2 and Tier 3, there are measured differences in proactive, automated regulatory monitoring (10% to 34%) and the prioritization of indirect tax as a funded IT roadmap investment (32% to 57%).

03

The difference between Tier 3 and Tier 4:
dedicated architecture and faster response

At the top of the tiers, the architecture changes. The most capable tier is the only one where a dedicated tax engine that other systems call for every transaction is the most common answer (49%, against 26% at Tier 3). Tier 4 IT leaders are almost three times as likely to absorb a new mandate in under a month (27% vs. 10%), and two-thirds (68%) say indirect tax was scoped in from the start of their most recent major systems initiative, against 47% at Tier 3. Standing board attention more than doubles again, from 24% at Tier 3 to 54% at Tier 4.

Finding 5 · Capability lens

The market’s AI vocabulary outruns its AI

Nearly every enterprise can date its “first AI agent” in indirect tax. Far fewer list AI agents among the tools they use, fewer still name agents as their most advanced AI in tax, and fewer than half of first-agent workflows do the two things that define an agent as described for respondents.

Respondents were given a definition before they answered: an AI agent is software that can take multi-step actions on its own — retrieving data, applying rules, and completing a task within a defined workflow — rather than only answering questions or generating text for a person to act on.

Under this definition, nearly all respondents (93%) can provide a go-live date for their organization’s first AI agent in an indirect tax or compliance workflow. Fewer (38%) list AI agents among the technologies they use, and 22% name agents as the most advanced AI in their tax workflows. Yet, when asked what the agent in their first workflow does without a person starting or finishing the step, 45% say it moves to the next step unprompted and 49% say it writes the result back into the system of record. Both are needed to meet the definition respondents were shown, so no more than 45% of those first-agent workflows meet it.

This is not a finding about enterprises overstating their capability. It is a finding about a category that has not agreed what an agent is. The vocabulary arrived from vendors — including this one — and buyers inherited a word without a stable definition. Readers should weigh this definitional confusion before taking self-reported AI adoption figures in this category at face value.

Sidebar 4 — External context

We are not the only ones who noticed

A vendor reporting that its own category has not agreed what a word means invites a fair question about motive. The observation is not ours alone.

In February 2026 the OECD published a working paper on the agentic AI landscape, informed by a workshop of more than 190 experts and delegations from sixteen countries. Its opening finding is that “AI agents” and “agentic AI” are used interchangeably across industry, creating significant conceptual confusion with consequences for governance, procurement, and risk management.

Regulators have met the same difficulty. The EU AI Act defines an “AI system” at Article 3(1); in July 2025 the European Commission issued separate guidelines on applying that definition because determining scope had proved difficult enough to need them. None of these sources measures the gap in a specific domain. That is what Exhibit 12 attempts.

This is external context describing the environment enterprises operate in. It is not a finding of this study.

Finding 6 · Cross-lens

Neither lens is explained by company size

The largest enterprises are the most likely to sit in the least capable tier, and size does not differ significantly between board-engaged and board-absent enterprises. Whatever separates the leaders, it is not scale.

On the capability lens, enterprises with 10,000 or more employees make up 18% of those studied but 34% of the least capable tier — nearly twice their share — and are significantly under-represented at Tier 3 (11%). Enterprises with more than $1 billion in revenue show the same pattern: 71% of Tier 1 against 59% overall, and significantly fewer at Tier 3 (51%). Within the most capable tier, both are close to average.

On the governance lens, revenue and headcount do not differ significantly between the two groups: 54% of board-engaged and 61% of board-absent enterprises have more than $1 billion in revenue; 15% and 16% have 10,000 or more employees.

If scale drove capability, the largest enterprises would cluster at the top of the tiers. They cluster at the bottom. The study did not measure indirect tax spending, so it cannot say how much budget contributes, though a dedicated AI budget is one of the questions that defines the tiers. A benchmark showing that bigger companies do better offers enterprises nothing they can act on. A benchmark showing no advantage from scale points to how an enterprise prioritizes and runs indirect tax — including whether IT funds it as a strategic priority, which rises from 5% at Tier 1 to 85% at Tier 4 — rather than to its size.

Finding 7 · Cross-lens

Proving the position remains unsolved

About half of enterprises take the more conservative tax treatment when they cannot prove a position — and that share does not differ significantly by capability tier or board cadence. Governance and AI capability correspond to catching more errors internally; neither, as measured here, closes the Compliance Confidence Gap.

This report measures both sides of the Compliance Confidence Gap: how often enterprises take the more conservative treatment when they cannot prove a position, and how much of their reserves exist because the position cannot be readily demonstrated.

Finance and tax leaders were asked how often, when their team cannot confidently prove a determination would hold up at audit, their organization takes the more conservative treatment — treating an uncertain transaction as taxable, declining to claim an exemption, or not reclaiming eligible indirect tax. Overall, 52% do so always or most of the time, with no statistically significant difference by tier or by board cadence.

Reserves tell the same story. Nearly three-quarters of enterprises (74%) hold reserves or accruals against indirect tax exposure. Among them, 73% say at least a quarter of that reserve exists because the organization cannot readily demonstrate its position — not because the law is genuinely unclear. Enterprises that treat indirect tax as a standing board item are significantly more likely to put that share at half or more (38%, against 20% overall).

One reading is that board attention makes the Compliance Confidence Gap more visible without closing it. An outcome that is correct but cannot be proven is still paid for — in overpaid tax, unclaimed exemptions, and reserves carried on the balance sheet against positions that could have been defended.

What we could not explain

One pattern in the data has no settled explanation, and we publish it rather than leave it out.

The most capable enterprises build tax into major systems projects from the start — but not by bringing tax people in earlier.

Two-thirds of Tier 4 IT leaders (68%) say indirect tax was scoped in as a core requirement from the start of their most recent ERP migration, e-commerce platform, or finance transformation — significantly more than any other tier. Yet when asked at what stage tax stakeholders are typically brought into technology projects, these enterprises are no more likely than anyone else to say “at requirements and design” (35%, against 44% overall).

The developing tier (Tier 2) shows the reverse gap: it is significantly the least likely to have scoped tax in from the start (28%) and, directionally, the most likely to have added it later in the project (43%).

Four explanations fit the pattern, though none was tested with this survey:

IT owns the agenda.

At more capable enterprises, IT may carry enough indirect tax fluency to write the requirements without tax specialists in the room. It fits two other findings: 85% of the most capable IT leaders call indirect tax a funded roadmap priority, and Tier 4 most often scopes tax in from day one. The study did not ask who owns the indirect tax agenda.

The rules are already encoded.

A dedicated tax engine may carry the requirements, so tax input at the project level matters less. Tier 4 is the tier most likely to run one (Exhibit 11).

Project volume.

More capable organizations may run more projects, diluting the share where tax joins at requirements.

Definitions.

IT leaders at more capable organizations may hold a stricter view of what counts as the requirements stage.

What this means for your organization

The Index describes differences between organizations at one point in time. It does not establish that any practice causes any outcome. What it can inform is which posture an organization likely shares, and what enterprises one step further along have in common.

Four questions worth answering

When did indirect tax last reach your board or executive committee?

If the answer is “when something went wrong,” you share a posture with the 43% of enterprises where indirect tax reaches the board only when a problem arises, if at all — a group four times as likely to learn of its errors from outside.

Where do your indirect tax errors surface?

If you usually learn from a tax authority, an auditor, or a customer, you share that with nearly one in four enterprises in the least capable tier.

Where do your tax rules live?

Four in ten least-capable enterprises keep them spread across systems or hard-coded in ERP customizations and spreadsheets. Every rule change is multiplied by the number of places it must be made.

How long does one taxability change take to go live?

Measure it from the tax team’s decision to the change going live across every system. One in five enterprises in the least capable tier needs more than 30 business days. From the advanced tier upward, none does.

Three disciplines that separate the steps

Stop doing tax changes by hand.

Manual change-and-check is the clearest marker of the least capable tier (45%, against 13% one step up).

Put indirect tax on a governance cadence.

Standing board attention, proactive regulatory monitoring, and funded IT priority all rise sharply at the same step from developing (Tier 2) to advanced (Tier 3) enterprises.

Consolidate the rules, and prove benefit before you expand.

A dedicated tax engine is the most-capable tier’s signature; measured benefit before extending AI is the gate the least capable tier skips.

These are the practices that differ most between tiers, not steps guaranteed to move an organization between them.

Beyond the data: a Vertex perspective

The study's most persistent result is the one neither lens explains. About half of enterprises take the more conservative treatment when they cannot prove a position, whatever their board cadence or capability tier. Among those holding reserves, nearly three-quarters attribute at least a quarter of them to positions they cannot readily demonstrate. Board attention and AI capability correspond to catching more errors internally. As measured here, neither corresponds to proving more positions.

Our view is that proof cannot be assembled after the fact. It is a property of the determination itself: the rule applied, the data used, the jurisdiction and the treatment, recorded at the moment the decision is made. An organization that captures that record as it transacts has an audit-defensible source of tax truth before anyone asks for it. One that does not must reconstruct it under scrutiny.

That is the premise of the Decision-to-Defense approach: govern indirect tax and compliance end-to-end, so that every determination becomes an outcome to prove, defend, and improve. In practice, that means determining tax accurately at the point of transaction and maintaining a continuous audit trail of every compliance decision, so that the record is ready for audit at any moment. This is how Vertex approaches indirect tax and compliance today.

The two lenses

Governance lens.

Finance and tax respondents only (n=369), grouped by board or executive-committee cadence. “Board-engaged” = standing item or periodically; “board-absent” = only when a problem arises, or rarely/never.

Capability lens.

All respondents, grouped by an AI Capability Score (0–100) built from ten AI questions (listed in Exhibit 6). Tiers: 0–30, 31–54, 55–70, 71–100. No finding compares tiers on a score input.

Interpretive limits

  1. Cross-sectional: the study describes differences between organizations at one point in time. Relationships are written as “tend to,” never as cause.
  2. The two lenses overlap: board-engaged enterprises skew toward upper tiers (13% in Tier 1, against 34% of board-absent enterprises). Within tiers, the governance gap narrows but persists on several measures in the developing and advanced tiers; it largely disappears in the least capable tier, and the most capable tier is too small to read (board-absent n=9).
  3. Board-engaged respondents are more likely to be C-suite (40% vs. 29%); some differences may reflect who answered.
  4. The study measured where errors are identified, not how often or how quickly they occur.
  5. Small cells: Tier 4 n=34 on finance and tax measures; Tier 1 n=55–56 on IT measures; Tier 1 n=28 on the expansion-gate question; rarely/never board cadence n=35–37.
  6. AI capability findings rely on respondents’ descriptions of their software, not technical audit.
  7. Sidebars are external context from public sources, not findings of this study, and were current at the time of writing.
